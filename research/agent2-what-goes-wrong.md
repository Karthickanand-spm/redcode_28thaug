# What Actually Burns People in Contracts — Clause-Type Findings

Pre-PRD market validation research for Redline. Ranked by how often/severely each clause type surfaces in real complaints, lawsuits, regulatory actions, and firsthand accounts (not just legal-guide explainers). Search budget used: 12/12 web searches, 13/15 page fetches.

---

## 1. Auto-renewal / deliberately hard-to-cancel subscriptions

**Why it burns people:** Contracts silently roll over into new payment periods, and cancellation is made procedurally difficult (in-person only, mail-in forms, limited windows) so people keep paying for something they tried to quit.

**Evidence:**
- [FTC "Click-to-Cancel" enforcement coverage — Lexology, "LA Fitness and the FTC Square Off Over Gym Membership Cancellation"](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=39fa9ce2-0bd7-46d4-b9e5-e360308c99c3) — The FTC sued Fitness International (LA Fitness) alleging it violated the FTC Act and ROSCA by making sign-up trivial (auto-enrolled into monthly renewal) but requiring in-person visits during limited hours or certified mail to cancel.
- [Holland & Knight, "FTC Steps Up Subscription Enforcement After Click to Cancel Rule Struck Down"](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/09/ftc-steps-up-subscription-enforcement-after-click-to-cancel-rule) — Confirms the FTC continued pursuing negative-option/auto-renewal enforcement even after its formal rule was vacated in court, indicating this is an ongoing, high-volume category of real consumer harm.
- [WeConservePA Library, "Trapped in a Contract"](https://library.weconservepa.org/guides/185-trapped-in-a-contract) — Describes how auto-renewal (evergreen) clauses are routinely buried in dense terms, and consumers must proactively cancel before term-end or get locked into another full period.

## 2. Unilateral / no-notice termination clauses (accounts, services, ToS)

**Why it burns people:** The other party reserves the right to suspend or terminate the relationship "at any time," "without notice," "for any reason, or no reason" — leaving the user with no recourse when access, funds, or work product tied to the account disappears.

**Evidence:**
- [ToS;DR Case 201, "Your account can be deleted or permanently suspended without prior notice and without a reason"](https://edit.tosdr.org/cases/201) — Community-verified documentation that this exact clause language exists in the actual terms of 300+ real, major services including Discord, Amazon, PayPal, Uber, Coinbase, Airbnb, and Epic Games — meaning it is one of the single most common clause patterns people are actually bound by.
- Same source notes real consequence pattern: users can permanently lose usernames, purchased items, and accrued benefits with no compensation obligation on the provider's part.

*(Note: this category is under-represented in individual "I got burned" narratives in this search pass — the evidence here is strong on prevalence/documentation but thinner on named individual horror stories; a follow-up pass on r/legaladvice or account-suspension complaint threads would strengthen it.)*

## 3. Arbitration clauses / class-action waivers

**Why it burns people:** They don't cause a single bad event — they remove the ability to respond to one. Once signed, they block consumers from suing collectively or in open court, which is often the only economically viable way to challenge small-dollar, high-volume harms (fees, wage theft, discrimination).

**Evidence:**
- [Congress.gov CRS, "The Federal Arbitration Act and Class Action Waivers"](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12764) — Documents *AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion*, 563 U.S. 333 (2011), the real Supreme Court case that let companies enforce class-action waivers in consumer arbitration clauses nationwide, reshaping consumer recourse.
- [Keller Grover, "Arbitration Clause Inequities in Consumer Contracts"](https://www.kellergrover.com/news/consumer-protection/the-gross-inequities-in-arbitration-clauses-that-control-virtually-all-our-consumer-agreements/) — Reports roughly two-thirds of major credit card issuers use arbitration clauses specifically to prevent customer lawsuits, and frames the real-world effect: a company that takes $50 from a million customers faces a viable $50M class claim without a waiver, but effectively zero individual claims with one.
- [JAMS ADR, "Some Large Companies Are No Longer Requiring Consumers and Employees to Waive Class Action Claims"](https://www.jamsadr.com/insight/2022/some-large-companies-are-no-longer-requiring-consumers-and-employees-to-waive-class) — Confirms this is treated as a real, high-profile harm significant enough that some companies have publicly rolled the clause back.

## 4. Limitation-of-liability / damage caps

**Why it burns people:** When something does go badly wrong, a low pre-set liability cap (sometimes a token dollar figure) can leave the injured party absorbing the vast majority of real, provable losses — even after they win in court or arbitration.

**Evidence:**
- [ASCE Civil Engineering Source, "Limitation of Liability Clauses Are Like Kryptonite"](https://www.asce.org/publications-and-news/civil-engineering-source/civil-engineering-magazine/article/2021/12/limitation-of-liability-clauses-are-like-kryptonite) — Cites three real, named cases: *Zirkelbach Construction Inc. v. DOWL LLC* (contractor incurred $1.2M in damages, recovery capped at $50,000 — about 8% of the designer's fee); *Taylor Morrison of Colorado Inc. v. Terracon Consultants Inc.* (jury awarded $9.5M, capped down to $550,000); *Johnson Nathan Strohe P.C. v. MEP Eng'g Inc.* (architect found liable for $1.2M, but could only recover $252,720 from its engineer due to the engineer's own liability cap).

This is the single strongest sourced evidence in this research — three named cases with concrete dollar figures showing the cap materially changed who absorbed six- and seven-figure losses.

## 5. Indemnification clauses

**Why it burns people:** The signer agrees to cover the other party's legal costs and liabilities arising from the work — turning what looks like boilerplate into open-ended personal financial exposure with no cap, especially dangerous for freelancers who lack an employer's legal backing.

**Evidence:**
- [Poynter, "Indemnity clauses leave freelancers open to lawsuits"](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2015/indemnity-clauses-leave-freelancers-open-to-lawsuits/) — Real case: Forbes contributor Dolia Estevez was sued (defamation, IIED, business interference) over a 2013 article. Forbes's freelance contract required contributors to "indemnify Forbes and hold [it] harmless against any liability, cost or expense," so instead of Forbes defending her as it would a staff writer, Estevez was left to find her own (ultimately pro bono) legal counsel while the suit proceeded.
- Same article describes a related pattern: "professional litigants" have specifically targeted independent freelance writers with low-merit lawsuits in local courts, extracting nuisance settlements — a scam that works precisely because the freelancer's own contract has already signed away employer protection via the indemnification clause.

## 6. Non-compete clauses (employment and freelance)

**Why it burns people:** Restricts where/for whom someone can work after the relationship ends, sometimes for years, sometimes even when the clause is legally unenforceable — and workers/freelancers often comply anyway out of fear rather than legal necessity.

**Evidence:**
- [Colleen Doran, "The Non-Compete Clause" (Substack)](https://colleendoran.substack.com/p/the-none-compete-clause) — Real, firsthand-reported cases from freelance illustrators/creators in publishing who turned down paying work repeatedly, and even sought after-the-fact permission from former clients, because they were intimidated by non-compete language — including one contract attempting a 7-year restriction covering public appearances, teaching, and lecturing. The author states the clause "effectively put some freelancers out of work" even though it was legally unenforceable, because the freelancers didn't know that.
- [FTC, "FTC Announces Rule Banning Noncompetes" (April 2024)](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/ftc-announces-rule-banning-noncompetes) — Regulatory confirmation that non-competes were judged to impose "unnecessary, onerous, and often lengthy restrictions" on workers' ability to find new employment, serious enough to justify a (later vacated) nationwide ban attempt.

## What I could not find (weak or no real-world evidence)

- **IP assignment clauses (freelance work-for-hire disputes).** Multiple sources (flag.red, PatentPC, Aaron Hall's law blog, Creative Business News) all describe the *risk* of clients claiming ownership of freelancer work via assignment-vs-license confusion, and all of them frame their central example as an explicit hypothetical ("A designer finishes a brand identity project…") rather than a documented real dispute. I could not verify a single named real case or complaint thread for this category despite four separate fetches — this is a plausible pain point argued consistently across the legal-guide industry, but the evidence base here is inference from professionals, not sourced incidents.
- **Rent/fee escalation clauses (leases, CAM charges).** Search and fetch results (rent.com, Barnes Walker, jdelaw.nyc, tenant-rights.com) were consistently generic explainer content ("3% sounds small until it doubles your payments over 10 years") with no real tenant or business complaint attached. One source's article page explicitly stated it had no case study despite claiming the author had seen such disputes "in practice." Two fetch attempts on tenant/landlord-specific complaint pages 403'd.
- **Unilateral/hard-to-cancel leases (early termination penalties).** A targeted Reddit search (r/legaladvice) returned no indexed real threads — only legal-clause-database results (Law Insider). Likely exists as a real pain point but not evidenced within this search budget.
- **Vendor/SaaS liability disputes specifically in a SaaS (not construction/engineering) context.** The strongest liability-cap evidence found was in commercial construction/design contracts, not SaaS vendor contracts specifically — the SaaS-specific searches returned only generic negotiation guides, no named disputes.

---

## Ranking methodology

Ranked primarily by: (1) whether a *real, named* situation (lawsuit, regulatory action, individually reported firsthand account) was found versus only hypothetical/illustrative guide content; (2) documented scale/prevalence (e.g., ToS;DR's 300+-service documentation, "two-thirds of credit card issuers"); (3) severity as described in the source (dollar figures, loss of livelihood, loss of legal recourse); (4) recency, favoring 2024–2026 enforcement/coverage where available (FTC gym-membership and non-compete actions are both within the last two years). Categories in the "could not find" section were searched with the same rigor but did not clear the bar of a sourced real incident within the 12-search/15-fetch budget, and are flagged rather than filled in with plausible-sounding but unverified claims.
